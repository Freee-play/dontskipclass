// The Philosophy

What is "the in-between moment"?

It's the five or ten minutes between the bell ringing and your next class starting. It's not free time to fill up your whole afternoon — it's a short, specific window that exists whether you use it or not.

DontSkipClass is built for exactly that window. Quick to load, quick to play, and easy to close the second the next bell rings. The goal was never to give you a reason to stay out longer — it's to make sure the time you already have doesn't go to waste.

Used right, a few minutes of a game between classes is a nice reset. Used wrong, "just five more minutes" turns into a habit of skipping the class itself — and that's the one thing this site was never meant to encourage.

The Science Behind Why Short Breaks Actually Help You Focus

3 min read

It feels counterintuitive: if you're trying to get more done, shouldn't you just keep working? Research on attention says otherwise. Psychologists call it directed attention fatigue — the idea that focusing on anything for an extended stretch slowly drains a limited mental resource, the same way a muscle tires from repeated use. Once that resource is depleted, your ability to concentrate, filter out distractions, and retain new information all drop, even if you don't consciously feel tired yet.

This is part of where the Pomodoro Technique came from — the classic productivity method of working in short focused bursts (traditionally 25 minutes) followed by a brief break. It's not a magic number, but the underlying principle holds up: brief, deliberate breaks between periods of focus consistently outperform pushing straight through, especially for tasks that require sustained attention like studying or problem-solving.

There's a related idea called Attention Restoration Theory, which suggests that certain kinds of breaks — ones that are mildly engaging but don't require hard mental effort — actually help that depleted attention resource recover faster than just staring at a wall or scrolling something stressful. A short, low-stakes distraction between classes isn't just "not working." For a lot of people, it's doing real cognitive work: letting the part of your brain that was just straining to focus actually reset before it has to do it again.

The catch, and it's an important one, is the word short. The research backing all of this is about brief resets, not extended breaks — the benefit curve doesn't keep climbing the longer you stay away. That's the whole idea behind the in-between moment this site is built around: long enough to actually reset, short enough that you're back in your seat when the bell rings.

How to Take a Break Without It Turning Into Skipping Class

4 min read

Almost nobody sets out to skip class on purpose in the middle of a quick break. It happens gradually — "just one more round" turns into two, then you're watching the clock wondering if it's even worth walking in five minutes late. A few small habits make a real difference in keeping a break a break.

Decide before you start, not while you're playing. The moment you're already in the middle of something fun is the worst possible time to make a judgment call about when to stop — your brain is actively working against you right then. Set a mental (or literal phone) timer before you open anything, not after.

Treat the walk to class as the real deadline, not the bell. If your plan is "I'll stop when the bell rings," you've already cut it too close — you still need time to close things out, grab your stuff, and actually get there. Give yourself a buffer that accounts for the walk, not just the countdown.

Anchor breaks to natural transitions. A break between classes has a built-in stopping point that a break in the middle of a lecture doesn't. Passing periods, lunch, and the couple of minutes before a class technically starts are all moments that were already going to end on their own — which makes them much easier to actually walk away from than an open-ended break with no natural edge to it.

None of this is about willpower being good or bad. It's just that some breaks are structured in a way that makes stopping easy, and others aren't — and a little bit of planning is usually the difference between the two.

What Are "Unblocked Games," and Why Do They Even Exist?

3 min read

Most schools run some form of content filtering software on their networks — tools that categorize websites and block entire groups of them, often including anything tagged as games, entertainment, or social media. This is usually a genuine, reasonable policy decision: keeping school devices focused on schoolwork during instructional time, and complying with regulations that require schools receiving certain funding to filter student internet access.

The trouble is, these filters work off of broad category lists and domain blacklists, which means they're a blunt instrument. A simple, harmless browser game and a genuinely distracting site can end up in the same blocked category with no real distinction between them. "Unblocked games" sites emerged largely as a response to that gap — collections of small, lightweight, browser-based games hosted on domains that aren't yet swept up in those broad category blocks, giving people a legitimate way to spend a genuinely free moment (like an actual break period) on something fun.

The games themeselves are almost always simple by design — built to run instantly in a browser tab with no download, no install, and no account, which is exactly why they fit the "quick five minutes between classes" use case so well in the first place. It's less about getting around anything and more about the fact that break time is, definitionally, time that isn't instructional — and having something low-effort and fun available for it isn't inherently at odds with taking school seriously the rest of the time.
